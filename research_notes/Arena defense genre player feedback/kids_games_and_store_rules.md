# Kid-Friendly Mobile Action/Collection Games: Parent & Kid Feedback, Store Rules, and Stealth-Learning / Accessibility Practices

Research date: 2026-10-11. Scope: a single-player, offline-capable action/collection game for ages 4–12 with coins, gems, random item drops, card draws (gacha), daily rewards, IAP and analytics, shipped by a small indie team.

Access note for the report writer: several primary pages (support.google.com, ftc.gov, commonsensemedia.org, fairplayforkids.org, kqed.org, gameaccessibilityguidelines.com) could not be fetched directly from this environment (DNS blocked). Findings from those sources rely on search-result snippets and secondary coverage, and are marked as such. Apple's developer pages were fetched directly.

## Key Question 1: Which monetization/engagement mechanics do parents most often complain about in kids' games, and which do they tolerate or praise?

### Takeaway
Parents' loudest complaints cluster around four things: ads (especially unskippable/fake-close ads), in-game nagging for paid memberships or currency, children not understanding that virtual currency is real money, and paid random items that feel like gambling. What they praise is almost the mirror image: a one-time price or no purchases at all, no third-party ads, offline play, text-light/voiced UI, and learning that is genuinely built into the play rather than bolted on.

### Cited Findings

**Complaints: aggressive membership / "pay to skip the grind" (Prodigy as the canonical case)**
- In Feb 2021, a coalition of 22 US child-advocacy and consumer groups led by the Campaign for a Commercial-Free Childhood (now Fairplay) asked the FTC to investigate Prodigy Math for deceiving educators/parents and targeting children with manipulative marketing; the complaint says Prodigy tells teachers/parents the game "is and always will be free" while aggressively marketing a Premium membership to children in-game — [Fairplay complaint announcement](https://fairplayforkids.org/feb-19-2021-advocates-to-ftc-prodigy-math-game-preys-on-kids-and-families/); [NBC News coverage](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/ncna1258294); [TODAY coverage](https://www.today.com/today/amp/tdna209523)
- Parent accounts in that coverage: a psychologist said her daughter began pestering her for a membership after playing in math class; another described the child's pressure as "an addiction," pointing to in-game purchases like pets and houses; advocates noted "used in your child's classroom" is the shortest path to parents' trust — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/ncna1258294); [Fairplay](https://fairplayforkids.org/prodigy-losing-equation/)
- The complaint also alleged Prodigy lacked substantiation for claims that the game boosts math ability — [Fairplay](https://fairplayforkids.org/feb-19-2021-advocates-to-ftc-prodigy-math-game-preys-on-kids-and-families/)
- Prodigy's response: it does not charge schools/teachers, all educational content is free, >95% of registered users have never paid, and it "scaled back membership 'ads' when students are choosing to play at school" — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/ncna1258294)
- Common Sense Media parent reviews of Prodigy (~490 parent ratings): parents describe "some basic math practice" but fault its "aggressive membership model," weak educational effectiveness and frustrating gameplay; many see it as "more of a money-making tool than a learning tool." Kid reviews (~647) say the app has declined, pointing to paywalls on features like pets and basic content. CSM lists it as 7+ with a privacy "pass" (no sale of personal info / no personalized ads) — [Common Sense Media Prodigy review](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/prodigy-kids-math-game); [Parent reviews](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/prodigy-kids-math-game/user-reviews/adult) (snippet-level; page not fetchable from this environment)
- CSM's Prodigy English review (7+) notes limited learning supports, that kids can easily get "credit" for wrong answers, and that the membership push is still an issue — [Common Sense Media Prodigy English](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/website-reviews/prodigy-english)

**Complaints: ads**
- A 2026 analysis of app-store reviews of kids' apps (commercial blog; methodology not verifiable because the page could not be fetched) claims ad complaints are ~35% of all 1-star reviews in the kids category, with reviewers describing unskippable 30-second video ads and fake close buttons that send children to the App Store — [Unstar blog](https://unstar.app/blog/kids-parenting-app-reviews-what-parents-complain-about-2026) (low-confidence source; treat the percentage as indicative only)
- Local news coverage of parents upset at adult/"R-rated" game ads appearing on kid-friendly sites/apps — [KOLD 2021](https://www.kold.com/2021/09/21/parents-concerned-about-r-rated-adult-game-ads-kid-friendly-sites); [Neowin](https://www.neowin.net/amp/raunchy-ads-invade-kids-apps/)

**Complaints: virtual currency hides real cost; unauthorized spend (Roblox)**
- University of Sydney researchers (2025) interviewed children aged 7–14 about Roblox spending and found virtual currencies make it difficult for children to understand the real value of purchases, leading to overspending, disappointment, and family arguments — reported via [Anapol Weiss blog](https://blog.anapolweiss.com/blog/from-fun-to-financial-pressure-how-roblox-monetizes-young-players/) (law firm suing Roblox; advocacy source)
- A May 2026 FTC complaint by Fairplay and NCOSE against Roblox cites parents who found children had run up thousands of dollars without realizing real money was involved, targets "paid random items" (loot/mystery boxes) that advocates say mimic gambling, and argues limited parental controls lead to unintended spending; Roblox responded that it bans actual and simulated gambling — [Quartz](https://qz.com/fairplay-ncose-ftc-complaint-roblox-child-safety-052026)
- An App Store 1-star parent review quoted: "my daughter spent $240 in Robux without my permission" — via [Unstar](https://unstar.app/th/blog/roblox-minecraft-fortnite-kids-games-parent-complaints-2026) (low-confidence source)
- Peer pressure around avatar items/game passes is a recurring theme in commentary — [Anapol Weiss](https://blog.anapolweiss.com/blog/free-gaming-isnt-free-the-real-costs-of-letting-kids-play-roblox/)

**Complaints: daily-login pressure and gacha in Pokémon TCG Pocket**
- ScreenWise (parent-oriented review): "two free packs a day sounds generous until you realize it's designed to make kids log in daily"; flags trading as a safety red flag; recommends disabling IAP, discussing gambling mechanics, and monitoring trading; verdict "not a hard no, but it needs guardrails" — [ScreenWise](https://screenwiseapp.com/media/pokemon-tcg-pocket-app)
- UK primary school parent guide (#WakeUpWednesday) on TCG Pocket focuses on "addictive spending habits" and recommends monitoring purchases and managing play time — [Sandylands Primary](https://sandylands-primary-school.schudio.com/news/2025-10-31-what-parents-need-to-know-about-pokemon-tcg-pocket)

**Dark patterns: regulator and advocate framing**
- FTC's 2022 staff report "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" describes design choices that exploit cognitive biases to steer purchases/data decisions — [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/80648) (not fetchable; snippet)
- Epic Games/Fortnite (Dec 2022): $275M COPPA penalty plus $245M refunds for billing practices that tricked players into purchases; FTC cited a "counterintuitive, inconsistent" button layout that led to charges from a single press, and children making purchases without parental consent — [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/380803/epic-fined-520-million-over-dark-patterns-chil.html); [Fenwick analysis](https://fenwick.com/insights/publications/ftcs-aggressive-enforcement-of-childrens-privacy-and-dark-patterns-a-cautionary-tale-and-simple-steps-companies-can-take-to-reduce-risk)
- Advocates (Fairplay, CDD) asked the FTC in 2021 to prohibit dark patterns in the children's marketplace, citing children's "immature and developing executive functioning" — [Fairplay](https://fairplayforkids.org/?p=27091)
- Australian 2025 report (Consumer Policy Research Centre + Monash): 95% of adult players encountered manipulative design at least once; flags loot boxes, battle passes, skin betting as gambling-like — [APO](https://apo.org.au/sites/default/files/resource-files/2025-04/apo-nid330381.pdf); [Consumers Federation](https://consumersfederation.org.au/?p=37068)
- UK ICO Children's Code games guidance (16 Feb 2023) advises avoiding "nudge techniques" that push children into poor choices, with particular care around time-limited offers, and covers randomised rewards such as loot boxes; one hypothetical case study concerns a publisher receiving "numerous complaints from parents" about children's engagement with IAP — [Mishcon de Reya](https://mishcon.com/news/ico-publishes-new-top-tips-for-games-designers); [DLA Piper](https://mse.dlapiper.com/post/102i83a/new-ico-guidance-on-childrens-code-for-games-companies-identify-under-18s-and-p)

**Parent-guidance consensus on chance-based purchases for kids**
- Netsafe (NZ): chance-based purchases "are generally not recommended for children"; if allowed, set firm limits on frequency and amounts — [Netsafe](https://www.netsafe.org.nz/gaming/in-app-purchases-and-loot-boxes)
- German media-literacy initiative Schau Hin: for children under 12, discourage games with loot boxes or disable IAP on devices — [Games Market](https://www.gamesmarket.global/protection-of-minors-loot-boxes-are-dangerous-for-children-says-schau-hin-6223a9396ba4d13293a687900f244f8a/)
- Bitdefender parent guide warns of the "try again" pattern: a child who doesn't get the hoped-for item feels the urge to buy again — [Bitdefender](https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/how-to-explain-loot-boxes-and-in-game-purchases-to-kids)
- ESRB parent guidance: check the "In-Game Purchases" vs "Includes Random Items" labels; use platform purchase controls; set family house rules — [ESRB](https://esrb.org/blog/what-parents-need-to-know-about-loot-boxes-and-other-in-game-purchases/)

**Children's limited understanding of commercial intent**
- Secondary source attributes to the APA that children can't tell where games end and ads begin until ~age 8 (not verified against APA) — [AMA](https://www.ama.org/marketing-news/game-apps-are-the-latest-battleground-in-child-advertising/)
- Studies: very few children recognized the commercial intent of an advergame (Korean study); Dutch study found children who did understand commercial intent did not use that knowledge as a defense; a Czech thesis on Roblox/Fortnite found only ~two-fifths of children understood in-game brand marketing, improving with age — [earticle](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A141012); [UvA](https://dare.uva.nl/id/09486879-4558-4ec8-8c9f-35434b1b2b9a); [Charles University thesis](https://dspace.cuni.cz/handle/20.500.11956/191596)
- Ongoing Australian project studying what 8–9-year-olds understand about manipulative/deceptive practices in apps (no results published yet) — [Digital Child](https://digitalchild.org.au/research/research-projects/deception-and-manipulation-in-childrens-mobile-device-apps/)

**What parents praise**
- Toca Boca: apps sold at a single up-front price; CEO Björn Jeffrey said children should not be exposed to third-party ads or IAP and that parents' trust is central; design deliberately text-light and open-ended; gender-neutral — [Mobile Marketing Magazine](https://mobilemarketingmagazine.com/playing-safe-toca-boca/); [Funambulism interview](https://funambulism.com/2014/02/28/interview-with-bjorn-jeffrey-ceo-of-toca-boca/); [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/how-toca-boca-got-100m-mobile-downloads-by-putting-kids-first/) (sources are 2014–2016; Toca Boca's current model, e.g. Toca Boca World, includes IAP — flag as possibly outdated)
- PBS KIDS Games praised for "zero ads, zero in-app purchases, zero social feeds, and full COPPA compliance"; voiceover-led, low-text UI; predictable, instant visual feedback — [Educators Technology review](https://www.educatorstechnology.com/2026/09/pbs-kids-games-review.html); [ScreenWise on Cat in the Hat Builds That](https://screenwiseapp.com/media/the-cat-in-the-hat-builds-that-app); PBS KIDS VP on touch screens suiting young learners and multi-media learning — [KQED MindShift](https://ww2.kqed.org/mindshift/16046/pbs-launches-new-apps-and-games-for-home-and-school)
- Parent-blog recommendations: Khan Academy Kids, Duolingo ABC, PBS Kids Games as ad-free starting points; advice to "test with wifi off before handing over the tablet"; paid versions tend to be ad-free — [Lilach Bullock](https://www.lilachbullock.com/free-games-for-kids-to-play-offline-with-no-ads/)
- Sago Mini World praised for a calmer approach (softer sounds, slower animations) — [Ungrammary](https://ungrammary.com/post/designing-for-kids-ux-design-tips-for-children-apps)

### Inferences
- For a 4–12 game, the parent-acceptable monetization set is: premium one-time price, or a clearly labeled "full unlock"/cosmetic packs behind a parental gate, no third-party ads, no energy timers, no in-game nagging. Any paid random draw is the single highest-risk design choice reputationally, regardless of store legality.
- Random draws earned purely through play (no real-money path, no "buy gems to draw") avoid most of the gambling framing, but daily-login pack mechanics (TCG Pocket pattern) are now independently criticized as engagement manipulation; a "daily reward" should be a bonus, not the gate to progress.
- Parents are explicitly offline-testing apps; an offline-capable game with no ads is a differentiator worth stating on the store page.
- The Prodigy case shows that "educational" branding raises, not lowers, scrutiny of monetization: parents feel betrayed when a learning game nags for money.

### Gaps
- No systematic, peer-reviewed dataset of parent complaints by mechanic was found; the only quantitative claim (35% of 1-star reviews are about ads) is from a commercial blog whose methodology could not be verified.
- Reddit threads (r/Parenting, r/daddit, r/ScienceBasedParenting) did not surface in search; no direct forum quotes.
- Common Sense Media's editorial learning rating/verdict for Prodigy was not readable (page blocked); only aggregated parent/kid review summaries were available.
- Outcome of the 2021 Prodigy FTC complaint not found.

## Key Question 2: Apple's current Kids Category and loot-box rules; Google Play Families requirements; COPPA basics

### Takeaway
Apple's Kids Category bans links out, purchasing and "other distractions" unless behind a parental gate, bans third-party ads/analytics except in narrow documented cases, and requires odds disclosure for any purchasable randomized items; Google Play mirrors the odds rule and requires Families-self-certified ad SDKs plus no advertising-ID transmission for child audiences. COPPA was amended in 2025 with most obligations in force since 22 April 2026 (written data retention and security programs, expanded personal-info definition).

### Cited Findings

**Apple App Store Review Guidelines (fetched directly 2026-10-11)**
- Guideline 1.3 Kids Category: "These apps must not include links out of the app, purchasing opportunities, or other distractions to kids unless reserved for a designated area behind a parental gate. Keep in mind that once customers expect your app to follow the Kids Category requirements, it will need to continue to meet these guidelines in subsequent updates, even if you decide to deselect the category." — [Apple Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 1.3 on data: "Kids Category apps may not send personally identifiable information or device information to third parties. Apps in the Kids Category should not include third-party analytics or third-party advertising... In limited cases, third-party analytics may be permitted provided that the services do not collect or transmit the IDFA or any identifiable information about children (such as name, date of birth, email address), their location, or their devices. This includes any device, network, or other information that could be used directly or combined with other information to identify users and their devices. Third-party contextual advertising may also be permitted in limited cases provided that the services have publicly documented practices and policies for Kids Category apps that include human review of ad creatives for age appropriateness." — [Apple Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 3.1.1 IAP: in-game currencies, levels, premium content, full-version unlocks must use Apple IAP. Loot boxes verbatim: "Apps offering 'loot boxes' or other mechanisms that provide randomized virtual items for purchase must disclose the odds of receiving each type of item to customers prior to purchase." — [Apple Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.4 Kids: apps may ask for birthdate/parental contact info only to comply with COPPA/GDPR etc. and must still provide functionality regardless of age; "Apps intended primarily for kids should not include third-party analytics or third-party advertising"; Kids Category apps or any that collect/transmit personal info from a minor must include a privacy policy; "the parental gate requirement for the Kid's Category is generally not the same as securing parental consent to collect personal data under these privacy statutes." — [Apple Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 2.3.8: terms like "For Kids"/"For Children" in metadata are reserved for the Kids Category; apps outside it cannot imply a child main audience in name, subtitle, icon, screenshots or description. Icons/screenshots/previews must adhere to a 4+ rating regardless of app rating. 2.3.6: answer age-rating questions honestly so the app "aligns properly with parental controls." — [Apple Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Apple Kids Category page: choose one age band in App Store Connect (5 and under; 6–8; 9–11); follow 1.3 and 5.1.4; provide parental gates. Parental gates are "adult-level tasks that must be completed in order to continue," to prevent buying IAP or following link-outs (websites, social networks, other apps) without a parent; for pre-literate kids "consider a voiceover prompt telling kids they need to involve their parent"; example gates shown include a math task and a question. Ads must be "human-reviewed for age appropriateness in order to be displayed." Kids apps "should not transmit personally identifiable information or device information to third parties — even in sections intended for adults — unless the parent explicitly consents." Children can use Ask to Buy to send a purchase request to a parent. — [Apple Kids Apps](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/kids-apps/)
- Age ratings overhaul (announced July 2025, iOS 26): new 13+, 16+, 18+ tiers replace 12+/17+, alongside 4+ and 9+; rating is computed from a required App Store Connect questionnaire; developers may choose a stricter rating; ratings can now differ by region; existing apps were reclassified automatically and developers had until 31 Jan 2026 to complete the new questionnaire — [iPhone in Canada](https://www.iphoneincanada.ca/2025/07/25/updated-app-store-age-ratings); [PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/apple-overhauls-app-store-age-ratings-with-new-categories); [Apple Kids Apps](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/kids-apps/)
- Ask to Buy (Family Sharing): child requests go to the organizer/parent for IAP, app downloads, and redownloads; approved items download automatically; on by default for under-13s (and in some regions cannot be turned off for under-18s); once turned off for an 18+ member it cannot be re-enabled — [Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-gu/105055); [Apple Support HT201089](https://support.apple.com/HT201089/)

**Google Play Families**
- Odds disclosure (Monetization and Ads policy, since May 2019): "Apps offering mechanisms to receive randomized virtual items from a purchase (i.e. 'loot boxes') must clearly disclose the odds of receiving those items in advance of purchase." — [Fenwick](https://fenwick.com/insights/publications/google-play-now-requires-disclosure-of-loot-box-odds); [PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/google-play-developers-must-now-disclose-loot-box-odds)
- Ads: if the target audience is only children, the app must use only Families self-certified ads SDK versions; mixed audiences must implement age screening (e.g. a neutral age screen) and ensure children only see ads from self-certified SDKs; self-certification is not required for in-house cross-promotion but the developer remains responsible for content and data compliance; certified SDKs must rate creatives into at least "Everyone" and "Mature" groups — [Families Self-Certified Ads SDK Policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/12918983) (snippet; page not fetchable); [AdMob guidance](https://pss.support.google.com/admob/answer/6223431)
- Identifiers: apps targeting children or users of unknown age must not transmit certain identifiers including the Android Advertising ID; GMA SDK 20.6.0+ (Android) / 7.67.0+ (iOS) suppress AAID for child-directed requests; the AD_ID permission can be excluded from the manifest to disable it app-wide — [AdMob](https://pss.support.google.com/admob/answer/6223431)
- Google states it is "the developer's responsibility to ensure the app satisfies all requirements concerning advertisements, in-app purchases, and commercial content" — [Google Play Families policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9893335) (snippet)
- Designed for Families program (2015 origins): required content equivalent to ESRB E or E10+, disclosure of interactive features/third-party data sharing/location, placement in a Families category (Action & Adventure, Brain Games, Creativity, Education, Pretend Play, Music & Video), IAP allowed but no "overly aggressive commercial tactics" — [GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2015/googles-designed-for-families-program-will-highlight-kid-friendly-android-apps/); [EdSurge](https://edsurge.com/n/2015-04-14-google-launches-designed-for-families-program-to-vet-child-appropriate-apps) (historical; current policy text not retrievable)
- Google Play Purchase Requests: parents can share a family payment method with under-13s and approve/decline each purchase; children can send Purchase Requests when no family payment method is set; later expanded to all family members — [Google blog](https://blog.google/products/google-play/purchase-requests-google-play-families/); [Android Central](https://www.androidcentral.com/apps-software/google-play-purchase-requests)

**COPPA (US)**
- Amended COPPA Rule published in the Federal Register 22 April 2025, effective 23 June 2025, with most operators given until 22 April 2026 to comply (safe-harbor programs had an earlier 22 Oct 2025 deadline for new disclosures); that deadline has passed — [BBB National Programs](https://bbbprograms.org/media/insights/blog/coppa-amended); [Toy Association](https://toyassociation.org/PressRoom2/News/2026-News/updated-coppa-rule-requirements-take-effect-april-22.aspx); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/data-protection/1661226/amendments-to-the-coppa-rule-now-in-effect)
- New/expanded requirements: personal information now includes biometric identifiers (face templates, fingerprints, retina scans, voiceprints), government IDs, phone numbers, audio recordings, certain geolocation; operators must have written data retention and information security programs specific to children's data and publish the retention policy in the privacy notice; audit vendor agreements for security assurances; train staff; FTC indicated further rulemaking on age verification is forthcoming — [BBB National Programs](https://bbbprograms.org/media/insights/blog/coppa-amended); [Mondaq](https://webiis10.mondaq.com/unitedstates/privacy-protection/1790866/coppas-amended-rule-is-now-in-full-effect-what-operators-need-to-know); [Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/fr/insights/publications/2025/05/childrens-online-privacy-in-2025-the-amended-coppa-rule)
- Enforcement benchmark: Epic Games paid $275M for COPPA violations (collecting data from under-13 Fortnite players without parental consent, default-on voice/text chat) plus $245M in dark-pattern refunds — [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/380803/epic-fined-520-million-over-dark-patterns-chil.html); [Fenwick](https://fenwick.com/insights/publications/ftcs-aggressive-enforcement-of-childrens-privacy-and-dark-patterns-a-cautionary-tale-and-simple-steps-companies-can-take-to-reduce-risk)

**UK (relevant if shipping globally)**
- ICO Children's Code games guidance (Feb 2023): risk-assess, identify under-18 players, avoid nudge techniques, special care with time-limited offers and randomised rewards — [Mishcon de Reya](https://mishcon.com/news/ico-publishes-new-top-tips-for-games-designers); [Wiggin](https://www.wiggin.co.uk/insight/the-ico-publishes-final-guidance-on-likely-to-be-accessed-by-children-factors-to-consider-for-uk-age-appropriate-design-code/)

### Inferences
- Practical indie checklist implied by the rules: (1) all IAP and any external links behind a parental gate that is a genuine adult task (and voiced for pre-readers); (2) no third-party ad SDK at all — simplest way to satisfy both stores; (3) analytics only if first-party or a vendor with documented Kids-Category practices that never sends IDFA/AAID, device identifiers, or location — for an offline single-player game, local-only or fully anonymous aggregate analytics is the lowest-risk path; (4) if any randomized item can be bought with real money or with a currency purchasable for real money, publish per-rarity odds in-UI before purchase on both stores; (5) a privacy policy that addresses children, retention and security, even if you collect nothing; (6) answer Apple's new age-rating questionnaire truthfully — random-item purchases and IAP are questionnaire items.
- Apple's "once customers expect Kids Category compliance you must keep complying" clause means do not enter the Kids Category experimentally and later add ads/links.
- The Apple parental gate ≠ COPPA consent distinction matters: the gate solves store review, not legal consent to collect data.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch the live Google Play Families policy text (support.google.com blocked), so exact current wording on IAP, "Teacher Approved," and 2025–2026 updates is unverified; the Designed for Families details cited are from 2015 coverage.
- Could not fetch FTC's COPPA FAQ directly; "support for internal operations" exception details and verifiable parental consent methods are not covered here.
- Apple's guidelines page was read only up to ~Guideline 5.3; nothing beyond that was checked. No "last updated" date was present on the page.
- Whether Apple's questionnaire disallows the Kids Category for apps that declare "paid random items" was not confirmed (see KQ3).

## Key Question 3: Do random item drops and card draws push a kids' game out of the Kids Category or create ratings issues (ESRB "In-Game Purchases (Includes Random Items)")?

### Takeaway
Nothing found in Apple's published rules bars randomized rewards from the Kids Category as long as any *paid* randomization is behind a parental gate and odds are disclosed; the ESRB "Includes Random Items" descriptor applies only when randomized items can be acquired with real money (directly or via purchasable currency). Earned-only random drops carry no store or rating penalty, but any real-money path to draws triggers both the odds rule and the ESRB descriptor, and invites the gambling framing parents and regulators now apply.

### Cited Findings
- ESRB introduced the "In-Game Purchases (Includes Random Items)" interactive element in April 2020 for games where purchases include any randomized elements: loot boxes, gacha, item or card packs, prize wheels, treasure chests; the plain "In-Game Purchases" notice continues to cover non-random purchases such as skins, music, virtual coins/currency, subscriptions, season passes, upgrades — [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/esrb-adds-a-new-warning-label-for-loot-boxes); [ArentFox Schiff](https://afslaw.com/perspectives/the-fine-print/esrb-launches-new-warning-label-video-game-loot-boxes); [CGMagazine](https://www.cgmagonline.com/news/esrb-announces-new-label-signifying-loot-boxes)
- ESRB avoided the term "loot box" because fewer than a third of parents both had heard of a loot box and knew what it was — [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/esrb-adds-a-new-warning-label-for-loot-boxes)
- A law-firm analysis argued the label alone likely will not reduce regulatory scrutiny of loot boxes — [ArentFox Schiff](https://afslaw.com/perspectives/the-fine-print/esrb-launches-new-warning-label-video-game-loot-boxes)
- Apple's odds rule applies to "mechanisms that provide randomized virtual items for purchase" — i.e. tied to purchase, not to earned drops — [Apple Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/); Google's wording likewise: "randomized virtual items from a purchase" — [Fenwick](https://fenwick.com/insights/publications/google-play-now-requires-disclosure-of-loot-box-odds)
- Apple Kids Category rules say nothing specific about random rewards; the constraints are on purchasing opportunities (gate) and data — [Apple Kids Apps](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/kids-apps/)
- Research on adolescents: Zendle, Meyer & Over (2019, Royal Society Open Science) surveyed 1,155 16–18-year-olds and found loot-box spending linked to problem gambling (η² = 0.120), more than twice as strong as in adults; correlational; motivations included fitting in via skins and "the enjoyable feeling of simply opening a box" — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/146026); [TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/study-shows-loot-boxes-linked-with-problematic-gambling-in-minors)
- Zendle & Cairns told UK Parliament that loot boxes "either literally cause problem gambling, or they exploit problem gambling amongst gamers," and that regulation is appropriate — [UK Parliament written evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/98535/html/)
- Fairplay/NCOSE's 2026 FTC complaint against Roblox specifically targets "paid random items" available to children as gambling-like — [Quartz](https://qz.com/fairplay-ncose-ftc-complaint-roblox-child-safety-052026)
- Netsafe and Schau Hin advise against chance-based purchases for children (under 12) — [Netsafe](https://www.netsafe.org.nz/gaming/in-app-purchases-and-loot-boxes); [Games Market](https://www.gamesmarket.global/protection-of-minors-loot-boxes-are-dangerous-for-children-says-schau-hin-6223a9396ba4d13293a687900f244f8a/)

### Inferences
- Design rule of thumb for a 4–12 game: keep random drops and card draws *earned-only* (play, quests, daily bonus). Do not sell gems that can be spent on draws. Then: no odds-disclosure obligation, no ESRB "Includes Random Items" descriptor, no Kids Category issue, and the strongest defence against "gacha for kids" complaints.
- If you must monetize, sell deterministic things (full unlock, specific character packs, cosmetics) — these fall under plain "In-Game Purchases" and the Apple gate requirement only.
- Even earned-only, consider showing drop odds in a "collection book" anyway: it is cheap, teaches probability (fits an educational framing), and pre-empts "hidden odds" criticism.
- Daily rewards: keep them a bonus, not required for progression; avoid streak-loss punishment, which the ICO guidance on nudges/time-limited offers and the TCG Pocket critique both target.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm whether Apple's 2025 age-rating questionnaire treats "paid random items" as a factor that raises the minimum rating or disqualifies Kids Category selection; Apple's age-ratings reference page was not fetched.
- No source found on ESRB/IARC treatment of *earned-only* random drops (presumably none, since the descriptor is purchase-tied, but not explicitly confirmed).
- Zendle's study covers 16–18-year-olds, not 4–12; no equivalent quantitative study on young children and loot boxes was found.

## Key Question 4: What do educational-game researchers and reviewers say makes learning content land in an action game without feeling like homework?

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced principle is "intrinsic integration": put the learning content inside the core mechanic the player must think about to succeed, rather than gating fun with quiz interruptions ("chocolate-covered broccoli"). Studies of Zombie Division showed children both learned more and chose to play the intrinsic version ~7x longer; follow-up work suggests the mechanism is attention — players learn what the game forces them to attend to.

### Cited Findings
- Habgood & Ainsworth (2011, Journal of the Learning Sciences), Zombie Division (math game for 7–11-year-olds): in one study children learned more from the intrinsic version under fixed time limits and spent 7 times longer playing it in free-time situations; the authors argue many educational games underperform because they use extrinsic reward structures ("gold stars for good performance") rather than building motivation into the game itself — [Habgood thesis, Nottingham](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/10385/1/Habgood_2007_Final.pdf); [Sheffield Hallam summary](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/); [Nottingham repository](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/23577510)
- Cutting & Iacovides (2022) replicated higher learning in the intrinsically integrated version and proposed an attentional mechanism: players focus on features the game task requires and ignore irrelevant information — [UniversityXP summary](https://universityxp.com/research/2023/9/20/learning-by-doing-intrinsic-integration-directs-attention-to-increase-learning-in-games)
- "Stealth learning"/"sneak teaching" games hide pedagogical content completely within mechanics so players perceive an entertainment game; related "stealth assessment" (Shute) embeds assessment invisibly in gameplay — [Designs for Learning 2016 papers](https://www.designsforlearning2016.aau.dk/digitalAssets/196/196362_dfl_short-papers-samlet_final-pdf..pdf); [UniversityXP on stealth assessment](https://universityxp.com/research/2023/3/31/stealth-assessment-and-digital-learning-game-design)
- Tension noted in the literature: students are less positive when they feel the learning content is prominent in the game — [Designs for Learning 2016](https://www.designsforlearning2016.aau.dk/digitalAssets/196/196362_dfl_short-papers-samlet_final-pdf..pdf)
- KQED MindShift feature on "the secret sauce" of great educational games discusses intrinsic integration and the chocolate-covered-broccoli problem (page could not be fetched; content known only from search listing) — [KQED MindShift](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/20765/whats-the-secret-sauce-to-a-great-educational-game)
- Prodigy's weaknesses as reported by CSM — kids can get "credit" for wrong answers, limited learning supports, heavy time in non-math fantasy loop — illustrate the extrinsic-integration failure mode (math as a toll booth between fun segments) — [Common Sense Media Prodigy English](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/website-reviews/prodigy-english); [CSM Prodigy parent reviews](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/prodigy-kids-math-game/user-reviews/adult)
- PBS KIDS Interactive: games target different learning styles and understanding improves when children use two or more media; games developed with educator/child-development input (secondary sources) — [KQED MindShift 2013](https://ww2.kqed.org/mindshift/16046/pbs-launches-new-apps-and-games-for-home-and-school); [Educators Technology](https://www.educatorstechnology.com/2026/09/pbs-kids-games-review.html)

### Inferences
- For an arena/collection action game, "fact pops" that interrupt play are the extrinsic pattern the research warns against. Lower-risk patterns consistent with intrinsic integration: make the fact *useful* (e.g. a creature's real-world trait determines its ability — nocturnal animal sees in dark arenas; a fact book entry unlocks a tactical hint), present facts at natural rest points (post-wave, collection screen, card reveal), keep them optional and voiced, and never gate rewards on quiz answers.
- A collectible "fact book" is well aligned with research: it attaches learning to the collection motivation the player already has, and reading it is player-initiated (no homework feel). Zombie Division evidence suggests effect size depends on whether the fact affects gameplay decisions, so purely decorative facts will produce less learning.
- Avoid overclaiming educational outcomes in marketing: Prodigy's unsubstantiated efficacy claims were part of the FTC complaint.

### Gaps
- Evidence base for intrinsic integration rests largely on one game (Zombie Division) plus one replication; no large multi-game meta-analysis was found in this search.
- No research located specifically on "fact pop-ups" or collectible fact encyclopedias in action games; the inferences above are extrapolations.
- Common Sense Media's own editorial criteria for "learning" ratings were not retrievable.

## Key Question 5: Accessibility and difficulty practices that help young kids (readability, no fail-state punishment, checkpoints, haptics/sound cues)

### Takeaway
Guidance converges on: design for a ~2-year age band, tap-only input with large targets away from the bottom edge, instructions by voice/animation rather than text, instant and predictable feedback, adjustable (or removable) difficulty and time pressure, and no punishment for failure. Microsoft's Xbox Accessibility Guidelines give the most concrete standards (change difficulty any time without losing progress; pause/extend/disable time limits; critical UI text at or below a lower-secondary reading level).

### Cited Findings
- Design for a narrow (~2-year) age band rather than treating 3–10 as one group; preschoolers need simpler, more visual interfaces — [UX strategies for young children](https://joydeeproni.notion.site/Mentorship-Resources-for-Aria-1cb83cdce7d980e29e91daba3856129a); [Ungrammary](https://ungrammary.com/post/designing-for-kids-ux-design-tips-for-children-apps)
- Young children reason less well than adults, so show the goal of each task clearly; use audio/visual cues and animated demos instead of text alone, especially for non-readers — [Ungrammary](https://ungrammary.com/post/designing-for-kids-ux-design-tips-for-children-apps); [NN/g Designing for Children](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/designing-children/)
- Children handle taps far more easily than swipes, pinches, double-taps, tilt/shake; they tap everything, so limit on-screen options; they expect an immediate reaction and need to be able to recover lost work — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/children-first-802bd12fe28c); [Ungrammary](https://ungrammary.com/post/designing-for-kids-ux-design-tips-for-children-apps)
- Sesame Workshop "Best Practices: Designing Touch Tablet Experiences for Preschoolers" (2012, based on 50+ studies): single tap is the most intuitive gesture; avoid hotspots along the bottom edge/lower corners where palms rest; register taps on touch-down rather than lift — [Joan Ganz Cooney Center](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/?p=19607); [Licensing International summary](https://licensinginternational.org/news/sesame-workshop-shares-tips-for-creating-touch-screen-content-for-preschoolers); [The Register](https://www.theregister.co.uk/2012/12/20/sesame_street_touch_apps_best_practice/)
- Thinkrolls designers: the younger the child, the larger the target (Fitts' Law); a third-party blog suggests minimum 48x48 dp buttons with ~64 px spacing (unverified against Sesame) — [UXPA Magazine](https://oldmagazine.uxpa.org/?p=11776); [Ungrammary](https://ungrammary.com/post/designing-for-kids-ux-design-tips-for-children-apps)
- Game Accessibility Guidelines (Basic tier, via secondary summary): offer a choice of difficulty level; ensure all settings are saved/remembered; use simple clear language and text formatting; include tutorials; on text display speed, "reading speeds and ability are so varied even within very specific age brackets" that messages should be dismissed on player action or allow replay/pause; ~14% of adults have a reading age below 11 — [Webbie summary](https://www.webbie.org.uk/blog/?p=115); [GAG why-and-how](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/why-and-how/) (GAG site not fetchable; primary wording unverified)
- Xbox Accessibility Guidelines: XAG 108 — players should be able to change difficulty at any time without losing progress; XAG 116 — time limits: pause, extend, or disable time pressure; critical UI text "should not require a reading ability that's more advanced than a lower secondary education (seven to nine years of school)," narrative text exempt — [Microsoft Learn XAG](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/guidelines); [Xbox dev docs](https://devdocs.xbox.com/build/game-principles/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines.md)
- PBS KIDS games: predictable mechanics with instant visual feedback when something fails (e.g. a bridge collapses) so failure is informative rather than punitive; voiceover and visual cues guide pre-readers — [ScreenWise](https://screenwiseapp.com/media/the-cat-in-the-hat-builds-that-app); [Educators Technology](https://www.educatorstechnology.com/2026/09/pbs-kids-games-review.html)
- Sago Mini: calmer pacing (softer sounds, slower animations) suits sustained attention in young kids — [Ungrammary](https://ungrammary.com/post/designing-for-kids-ux-design-tips-for-children-apps)
- DiGRA 2026 mapping study: player reviews foreground basic hearing/motor/visual provisions (subtitles, remapping, legible text) while advanced cognitive guidelines are largely absent from practice — [DiGRA](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/view/2833)

### Inferences
- Concrete practices for a 4–12 arena/collection game, supported by the above: (a) a "little kid" mode with no timer/wave pressure and auto-aim or tap-to-target; (b) no permadeath or lost loot on defeat — retry from the current wave, keep everything earned; (c) difficulty adjustable mid-run from the pause menu without losing progress (XAG 108); (d) every important state communicated three ways — sound, visual, haptic — never by text alone; (e) icons plus voiced labels on all menu items; narrative/fact text can be richer but critical UI should be readable by a 7-year-old; (f) big tap targets, nothing critical in bottom corners; (g) remember all settings; (h) short sessions by design (an arena wave or a card draw as a natural stopping point) rather than appointment mechanics.
- Haptics specifically: no source addressed haptic cues for children; the recommendation to use them derives from general multi-modal redundancy guidance, not child-specific evidence.

### Gaps
- No child-specific accessibility standard was found; GAG and XAG are general-audience documents. Direct GAG wording could not be retrieved.
- No published Toca Boca or PBS KIDS design guideline document was located (only interviews and third-party observations).
- Sesame Workshop's exact tap-target size figures were not retrievable.
- No research found on haptic feedback or sound-cue design for 4–8-year-olds specifically.
